Never look at the Sun through the telescope or finder without a certified solar filter covering the front of the telescope. It causes instant, permanent eye damage.

Observing the Sun

Danger: sunlight focused by a telescope blinds instantly and permanently. A single glance through the eyepiece or the finder is enough.

  • A certified solar filter (ISO 12312-2 or solar film such as Baader AstroSolar) is fixed over the front of the telescope. Never use eyepiece-side filters.
  • The finder scope is covered or removed.
  • Children are supervised the whole time.

More about observing the Sun safely

Aiming without looking: turn the tube until its shadow on the ground is as small and round as possible. Then it points straight at the Sun. On cloudy days the arrows use compass and motion sensor, so expect a few degrees of error.

    What the settings do

    Location
    Pointing

    “Top edge” suits a phone strapped along the tube or held like a pointer. Without camera the position comes from compass and motion sensor; set the eyepiece point on a bright star to make it accurate. With a periscope mirror or prism that turns the camera’s view over the top of the phone, the camera and Locate work in this mode too (when you start the camera, the app asks whether the optic mirrors the image).

    Camera

    You can also use a USB webcam or an HDMI capture device mounted on the telescope with its own lens. Field of view and eyepiece point are remembered per camera.

    Camera field of view

    Measured automatically on the first successful solve. Typical for main phone cameras: 60–75°. For gallery photos the app searches the field of view every time, since lens and crop can differ.

    Compass

    After a camera solve with the motion sensor on, the app compares the compass with the exact solved direction and offers to correct it. The correction is used in compass mode and shown at the bottom left while active.

    Telescope and eyepieces

    The selected eyepiece is used on the map and in the eyepiece view. Its apparent field is printed on it or in its specs: about 50° for Plössl, 60–70° for wide-angle, 82° and more for ultra-wide eyepieces. True field = apparent field × eyepiece focal length ÷ telescope focal length.

    How far a Newtonian turns the view depends on where the focuser sits on the tube and how you look in. Compare the eyepiece view with a star pattern in the eyepiece once and turn it to match. The view shows stars to magnitude 9, deep-sky objects, the Moon and planets.

    Backup

    Lists: observing list, starred objects and logbook. Everything adds all settings: telescope and eyepieces, location, pointing, camera and display. Importing adds what is missing and never deletes anything; settings in a backup replace the current ones only if you confirm. The CSV opens in a spreadsheet.

    Stars to magnitude 9 (Hipparcos) and deep-sky objects from d3-celestial (BSD); Locate matches the stars to magnitude 6. Sun, Moon and planets: Astronomy Engine (MIT). Everything runs locally in the browser.
