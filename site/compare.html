Finding objects with a manual telescope: the options

There are five common ways to find a faint object with a telescope you move by hand. They differ in what they cost, what has to be on the telescope, and what you have to do each night. Zenigo is one of them.

When Zenigo fits

  • You already have a telescope you move by hand and don’t want to buy hardware for it: a phone holder is enough.
  • You want more than the way to the target: which objects suit your aperture and are well placed tonight, an eyepiece view oriented like yours, the best of your eyepieces for each object, a plan and a logbook.
  • You use several telescopes: the phone moves from one to the next, and each holder only needs its offset set once.

When something else fits better

  • Encoders measure the mount itself. They don’t need a view of the sky between alignments, don’t drift while you push, and leave the phone free. If you observe often with one Dobson that a kit is made for, they are the most robust push-to.
  • StarSense Explorer is a complete package if you are buying a first telescope anyway: telescope, dock and app are made for each other, and there is little to set up: the phone is aligned with the telescope once, which Celestron calls quick and easy.
  • A GoTo mount drives to the object and follows it as the sky turns. That matters at high magnification on planets and for photography, where Zenigo, like any push-to, leaves the tracking to you.
  • Star hopping needs no battery and teaches you the sky. Many observers combine it with any of the above.

Zenigo’s limits

  • The phone camera has to see about ten stars, so a part of the sky without clouds, trees or the Moon in the image.
  • Between two Locates the motion sensor carries the position and drifts a little over long moves. Locate again near the target, or turn on Continuous.
  • The holder has to stay put. If it moves, set the eyepiece point again.
  • The phone on the telescope uses its battery: take a power bank on long nights.
