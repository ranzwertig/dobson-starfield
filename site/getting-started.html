Getting started

Your first night with Zenigo, from installing the app to the first object in the eyepiece. Plan about 15 minutes for the setup. The first three steps are best done at home, while you still have signal; then check the list before you head out.

  1. Install the app

    Open the app on your phone and add it to the home screen. Installed, it starts full screen and keeps working at a dark site without any connection.

    • iPhone: open the app in Safari, tap the share button, then “Add to Home Screen”.
    • Android: open the app in Chrome, tap the menu, then “Install app” or “Add to Home screen”.

    Start it once from the home screen while you are online. The message “Ready for offline use” confirms that everything is stored on the phone.

  2. Set your location

    Zenigo needs to know roughly where you are to turn star positions into directions for your telescope. Tap Quick settings (the top round button on the right) and then GPS next to Location. Allow the location request, or tap Edit and type in latitude and longitude yourself. A precision of a few kilometres is plenty.

    Observing somewhere else? Update the location before you start, otherwise every direction is off.

  3. Enter your telescope and eyepieces

    Tap Quick settings → All settings → Telescope. Under Telescope and eyepieces, enter:

    • Focal length of the telescope, for example 1200 mm.
    • Aperture, the mirror diameter: 200 mm for an 8-inch Dobson.
    • Your eyepieces with Add eyepiece: focal length and apparent field. The apparent field is printed on the eyepiece or in its specs, about 50° for a Plössl.
    • Your Finder (red-dot, Telrad or a finder scope such as 8×50) and, if you have one, the Barlow lens.

    Zenigo then shows the eyepiece field and Telrad rings on the map, an eyepiece view of what you should see, and in each object’s details which eyepiece suits it best.

Before you head out

  • App added to the home screen and opened once online
  • Location set
  • Telescope focal length, aperture and eyepieces entered
  • Phone charged, holder and a low-power eyepiece packed
  • Rotation lock on, screen brightness down
  • Checked Tonight in the menu: when it gets dark, where the Moon is, the seeing forecast
  • Planned a few objects for tonight with the List button (or “Plan tonight’s best objects”)

  1. Mount the phone on the tube

    Use any phone holder that clamps firmly to the tube, for example a bike or tripod clamp. The camera on the back of the phone has to look in the same direction as the telescope, roughly parallel to the tube. It doesn’t have to be exact, step 6 takes care of the rest. With a 3D printer you can build a phone holder for the finder shoe yourself.

    • The holder must not move when you push the telescope. If it does, the eyepiece point no longer fits.
    • Turn on your phone’s rotation lock. Pointing high up, the phone lies flat and would otherwise flip the screen between portrait and landscape.
    • Lower the screen brightness, or switch on Red light in Quick settings, to keep your eyes dark-adapted.

  2. Turn on Align and tap Locate

    Turn on Align (the compass button) first and leave it on all night. It lets the map follow the phone’s motion sensors. Before the first Locate, the compass gives a rough direction, often 10° or more off. After a Locate, the exact direction comes from the stars, and the gyroscope carries it along while you push the telescope.

    Point the telescope at a part of the sky with several bright stars, away from trees, houses and the Moon. Tap the large orange Locate button. The camera starts, takes a few frames and matches the stars it finds against the catalogue.

    The very first time, Zenigo also measures your camera’s field of view. That can take up to ten seconds; afterwards a solve usually takes about a second. When it worked, the status at the bottom left says “Solved” with the number of stars, and green circles mark the stars it recognised. If the compass was far off, Zenigo asks whether to correct it: tap Correct.

    The first Locate asks for access to the camera, and on iPhone the first tap on Align asks for motion and orientation access. Allow both. Declined by mistake? Camera and sensor access shows how to allow it again.

    “Only a few stars detected”? Wait until it is properly dark, aim at a brighter area or raise Frames to stack and Sensitivity in Settings → Camera & sensors → Capture. Or take a night photo: Camera button → Night photo uses your camera app’s long exposure. More in Troubleshooting.

  3. Set the eyepiece point

    Locate tells Zenigo where the camera looks. The phone never sits exactly parallel to the tube, so the telescope looks a little past that. You teach Zenigo this offset once, at the beginning of the night:

    1. Put your lowest-power eyepiece in (the longest focal length, for example 25 mm).
    2. Push the telescope to a bright star you can see with the naked eye and centre it in the eyepiece by hand. A star is better than a planet or a nebula: it is a sharp point.
    3. Don’t touch the telescope now. Tap Locate and wait for “Solved”.
    4. Tap Eyepiece point, then tap that star on the screen. The tap snaps to the nearest star and names it, so you can check it is the right one.

    From now on the orange crosshair marks where the telescope points, and the directions refer to the telescope, not to the camera. The offset is saved and stays valid as long as the holder doesn’t move, also on the next nights.

    Check it: push to a second bright star elsewhere in the sky and tap Locate. The crosshair should sit on that star when it is in the middle of the eyepiece. If it is clearly off, the holder has moved or the first star was not quite centred: set the eyepiece point again.

    No stars detected? Without the camera, set it on the map instead: with Align on, centre a bright star in the eyepiece, tap Eyepiece point and tap that star on the map. This corrects the compass and the height difference, accurate to about a degree near that star. With the camera it is much more precise.

  4. Choose a target

    Tap Find target (magnifying glass). The list shows what is well placed right now. The chips at the top are categories: My telescope, Good now and Popular together, for example, show the showpieces your telescope handles well that are well placed now. You can also search by name or number, for example “M13” or “Vega”, or tap an object on the map and choose Set as target.

    Good first targets: the Moon, Jupiter or Saturn, the Pleiades (M45), the Andromeda Galaxy (M31) and the Hercules cluster (M13). The Target button shows everything about the current target in one panel.

    Plan the night with the List button: plan objects for tonight, then work through them with Next and log each one with Seen.

  5. Push to the target

    The two boxes at the top tell you what to do: how many degrees to turn left or right (azimuth) and to tilt up or down (altitude). The red arrow on the map points the same way.

    • With Align on, the numbers follow the telescope live while you push, using the phone’s motion sensor.
    • The motion sensor drifts a little over long moves. Close to the target, tap Locate again for an exact position, or turn on Continuous so it re-solves by itself.
    • Choose the eyepiece you are using in Quick settings → Eyepiece. When the target is well inside its field (within 70 % of the field’s radius, or 0.4° if you haven’t entered eyepieces), the top line says the target should be in the eyepiece. Look through a low-power eyepiece first.

Good to know

  • After a solve with Align on, Zenigo can offer to correct the compass. That makes the map more accurate when you start without stars, for example at dusk.
  • Push without looking at the screen: with a target set, tap Sound. Clicks tell you which way to turn, chirps which way to tilt, a chord says it’s in the eyepiece.
  • Plan with the observing list and tick off what you’ve seen in the logbook. With many objects, switch the order to Short route: the telescope moves as little as possible. Set your mount type in Settings → Telescope.
  • The bar under each object shows tonight from sunset to sunrise: amber while it is up, green while it is well placed.
  • Tap the Moon or a planet for more: craters at the terminator, Jupiter’s moons and their transits, Saturn’s ring tilt, and how big it looks in your eyepiece. Tonight in the menu lists eclipses, close pairs and meteor showers coming up.
  • Back up your lists and settings now and then: Settings → General → Backup and restore. Everything is stored only on your phone.
  • Use Filter to show only planets, only deep-sky objects or what you can see with the naked eye.
  • The settings you change at the telescope are in Quick settings, the top round button: eyepiece, eyepiece point, compass correction, red light, solving a photo, camera, pointing, what the map labels and location.
  • No camera on the tube? Make the top edge of the phone the pointer. A USB webcam can replace the phone camera.
  • On a tablet or computer, panels open at the side over the map, and the buttons move next to them.
  • Want to observe the Sun? Read the safety notes first. Never look at the Sun without a certified filter on the front of the telescope.

Everything else, every button and every setting, is explained in the Help.
