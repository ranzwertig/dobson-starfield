Your phone shows your telescope the way.

Mount the phone on the telescope. Zenigo recognises the stars in the camera image and tells you how far to turn and tilt until your target is in the eyepiece. For telescopes you move by hand: Dobsonians, refractors and Newtonians on alt-az mounts, travel scopes on a photo tripod, binoculars on a mount.

Open Zenigo Getting started

Free. Runs in the browser on phones, tablets and computers, no account, no download. Zenigo Pro is optional.

How it works

  1. Mount the phone on the tube

    Any holder that keeps the phone steady and its camera looking along the tube will do. Centre a bright star in the eyepiece once and tap it on screen, so Zenigo knows where the telescope points relative to the camera.

  2. Tap Locate

    The app takes a short exposure, finds the stars in it and matches them against a star catalogue. That is plate solving, and it takes about a second.

  3. Follow the arrows

    Pick a target. The numbers at the top tell you how many degrees to turn and tilt. The motion sensor keeps them up to date while you push.

Which telescopes

Anything you move by hand, with a place for the phone that moves with it. The directions are “turn” and “tilt”, the two axes of an alt-azimuth mount.

DobsoniansTurn on the base, tilt in the rocker box: exactly the two numbers Zenigo shows. Refractors and Newtonians on alt-az mountsManual heads, fork mounts, GoTo mounts with the clutches released. Travel scopes and MaksutovsOn a pan-tilt or video head; the phone sits on the telescope, not the tripod. Binoculars on a mountOn a tripod or parallelogram, with the phone on the same bracket.

No telescope?

Hold the phone up to the night sky and Zenigo names the stars, constellations and planets in front of you. With the camera it checks the view against the stars themselves, so the names sit on the right stars, not a few degrees off like a compass alone. Tap anything for details, see when the ISS comes over, and what tonight has to offer.

Zenigo as a star finder

At the telescope

Precise, without extra hardware
No encoders, no Wi-Fi box. The position comes from the stars themselves, accurate to a fraction of a degree, and the motion sensor keeps the directions up to date while you push.
Sky map that follows the phone
Without the camera you get a star map. Turn on Align and it moves with the phone, roughly by compass or precisely after a locate. Filter it to planets, deep-sky classes or what the naked eye sees, with constellation and star names as you like them.
See what the eyepiece shows
Enter your telescope, finder and eyepieces (with a Barlow lens, if you have one): the map shows your finder, Telrad rings and the true field, a round eyepiece view shows the stars around your target the way your Newtonian turns them, and every object tells you which of your eyepieces suits it best.
Your target at a glance
One panel with how far to turn and tilt, when the target is best placed tonight, its brightness and size, and what you logged about it before. Or listen your way there: clicks to turn, chirps to tilt, a chord when it’s in the eyepiece.
Moon and planets up close
The Moon in its phase with the craters that stand out at the terminator tonight, Jupiter’s four moons and when they or their shadows cross the planet, Saturn’s ring tilt, Venus’ phase, and how big each planet looks in your eyepiece.
With or without the phone camera
Use a USB webcam on the tube, a mirror or prism over the top of the phone, or no camera at all: then the top edge of the phone is the pointer.
A second screen in your hand
Connect a tablet or another phone to the one on the tube: it shows where the telescope points and the way to the target, optionally with the telescope’s camera image. Directly over Wi-Fi or a hotspot, no server.
Made for a dark site
Added to the home screen, it starts, solves and keeps your logbook without any signal. One switch turns the whole app red, the map stays dark, and the colours of the night bars and markers are muted.

Plan and keep track

Plan the night
When it gets dark, the Moon’s phase, which planets and deep-sky objects are well placed tonight, Jupiter’s moons, eclipses and other events coming up, seeing and light pollution for your site. Every object shows a bar for the night: amber while it is up, green while it is well placed. A time slider shows the sky at any hour.
Observing list and logbook
Star what you want to see some day, plan tonight’s objects in the order they set or along the shortest way for your mount, work through them with Next and log each one with seeing, eyepiece and notes. Back it all up in the settings, also to move to a new phone.
2,000 deep-sky objects, 78,000 stars
All Messier objects, about 1,900 NGC and IC objects and stars to magnitude 9, more the further you zoom in; with Zenigo Pro the whole NGC and IC catalogues and stars to magnitude 11. Tap anything for details and links to SEDS, NASA, SIMBAD and Wikipedia.
ISS and satellites
With a connection the map shows the ISS and about 150 bright satellites, lit or in Earth’s shadow. Move the time ahead to catch the next pass.
Your own sky photos
Take a night photo with your phone’s camera app, a long exposure in night mode that shows far more stars, or pick a photo from your gallery. Zenigo solves it and labels what is in it, with the planets where they were when you took it; save or share the labelled copy.
On the device, synced encrypted
Camera images, location, motion data and your logbook are processed and stored on the device; the optional camera image of the live connection goes only directly to your other device. Optional sync between your devices is end-to-end encrypted: the server only ever sees a locked copy. No account, no tracking.

Questions

Does it need an internet connection?
No. Added to the home screen, the catalogue, the solver, the planning and the logbook all run on the phone. Only satellites and sync need a connection.
Can the phone look through the eyepiece?
No, it looks at the sky next to the tube: recognising the stars needs a field of at least about 10°. The offset to the telescope is set once with a bright star.
How accurate is it?
The camera’s direction is known to a fraction of a degree after a Locate; with the eyepiece point set well, the target lands in a low-power eyepiece.
Does it work from a town?
Usually. It needs about ten stars in the camera image, and the brighter stars show through light pollution. A night photo with the camera app sees far more.

Pointing at the Sun

Zenigo can guide you to the Sun, for example on a cloudy day. Never look at the Sun through a telescope or finder without a certified solar filter covering the front of the telescope. It causes instant, permanent eye damage. Cover or remove the finder as well.

Put it on your home screen

Zenigo is a web app. Installed, it opens full screen like any other app and works offline. The getting started guide takes you from here to the first object in the eyepiece.

Open Zenigo

iPhone

  1. Open Zenigo in Safari.
  2. Tap the share button, then “Add to Home Screen”.

Android

  1. Open Zenigo in Chrome.
  2. Tap the menu, then “Install app” or “Add to Home screen”.

Tablet or computer

  1. Open Zenigo in Chrome, Edge or Safari.
  2. Install it with the install icon in the address bar (Chrome, Edge) or File → Add to Dock (Safari on Mac).
