Phone holder for the telescope

Zenigo finds where your telescope points by photographing the sky with the phone camera. That works best when the phone sits on the telescope firmly and always in the same place. Here is how to build such a holder yourself from a few standard parts and one 3D-printed adapter, which you can adjust to your telescope and download right here.

A do-it-yourself project. This is a building guide, not a product: Zenigo doesn’t sell these parts and gives no warranty. You print, build and use the holder at your own risk. Check that everything sits tight before you move the telescope, so the phone can’t fall.

Why a rail in the finder shoe

A phone strapped to the tube with a bike or tripod clamp works, but it shifts a little whenever you push the telescope, and after every setup it sits somewhere else. Each time, the eyepiece point no longer fits and has to be set again.

Almost every telescope already has a precise, sturdy mount for small accessories: the finder shoe. A printed adapter slides into it like a finder and carries a Picatinny rail, the rail system known from riflescopes and camera rigs. A camera mount clamps onto the rail, and the phone holder screws onto its 1/4" camera thread:

  • Rigid: the shoe and the rail clamp with screws, nothing wobbles when you move the telescope.
  • Repeatable: the rail has a slot every 10 mm. The holder goes back into the same place, so the eyepiece point stays valid from night to night.
  • Quick: the adapter slides out of the shoe with the phone holder still on it. Nothing to strap or tape to the tube.
  • Expandable: the rail takes anything with a Picatinny clamp or a 1/4" camera thread, for example a red light, a dew heater controller, a second camera or a mirror.

Got a Telrad base on the tube? The adapter can also slide into its two dovetail slots instead of the Telrad. No suitable finder shoe at all? Then take the universal V-block: it sits on any tube and is held with two cable ties or hose clamps.

Why a Picatinny rail

There are several rail systems for small accessories. Picatinny suits this job best:

  • Fixed positions. A cross slot every 10 mm. A clamp always goes back into exactly the same place, so after taking the holder off and putting it back, the eyepiece point still fits. Rails without slots, like Arca-Swiss or the NATO rail on camera rigs, clamp anywhere, but you have to find the spot again each time.
  • A standard, not a brand. The profile is defined in MIL-STD-1913: every Picatinny clamp fits every Picatinny rail, whoever made them. Weaver mounts usually fit too.
  • Easy to get and cheap. Aluminium rails in any length, and camera mounts with a 1/4" screw for a few euros each, because the rails are common on camera rigs, airsoft and hunting optics.
  • Room for more. Each item clamps on its own and can move independently: the phone holder today, a red light or a second camera tomorrow, without taking anything else off.
  • Sturdy and small. About 21 mm wide and 10 mm high, aluminium and screwed down: stiff enough for a phone, light enough for any finder shoe.

Why not just screw the phone holder directly onto the shoe adapter with a 1/4" screw? It works, but a single screw lets the holder turn, and there is room for exactly one item.

Building it

  1. Print the adapter

    Choose your mount type below, set how far the rail may reach forward and back, and download the STL. Print it as it comes, slot face on the bed: no supports needed. PETG or ASA hold up better than PLA in the cold and in a hot car; 4 walls and 20–30 % infill are plenty.

    Enter the outer diameter of your tube. With these settings the V-block takes tubes up to 283 mm. A 230 mm tube rests on both flanks, with its two contact lines 30 mm apart. A flatter V (larger angle) suits thick tubes, a sharper one thin tubes. Pull the straps through the channels and around the tube, tight but without crushing anything.

    A Telrad base has a block at each end with a dovetail slot along the tube. The two dovetail keys of the adapter slide in like the Telrad’s own feet and are locked by the thumbscrews. The key profile is taken from “Telrad Mount for Green LED” by jeffrey892, the distance between the slots from “Telrad Mount for Orion Dobsonian” by CHenryInSD (both CC BY). Check them against your base before printing the long part: if the keys sit loose or tight, change the widths by a few tenths.

    Vixen/Synta shoes differ by a few tenths of a millimetre. Measure the slot of yours, adjust the dovetail and print a short test piece first (foot length 20 mm, no overhang).

  2. Screw the rail onto the adapter

    Slide the T-nuts of the rail into the open slot of the adapter, put the rail on top and screw it down. The hole spacing of the rail doesn’t matter, the nuts go wherever the holes are. Let the rail reach towards the opening of the tube.

  3. Mount the phone holder

    Clamp the camera mount onto the rail and screw the phone holder onto it. Lock it with the knurled ring so it can’t turn. The phone stands upright, its back camera looking towards the opening of the tube, roughly parallel to it. It doesn’t have to be exact.

  4. Set up Zenigo

    Keep Settings → Camera → Pointing on Camera. Then locate once and set the eyepiece point on a bright star. As long as nothing moves on the rail, it stays valid for the next nights too.

A long rail is a lever on the small clamping screw of the shoe. If it wobbles, add a strap around the tube at the far end of the rail. With the V-block, keep the rail’s overhang short or make the block longer.

Example: a finished holder

This is the holder from this page, built and mounted on an 8-inch Dobson whose finder shoe is the Explore Scientific type. The tube runs from left to right, its opening is to the right, out of the picture.

  • The adapter (1) is printed with a long base. Its foot slides into the telescope’s own finder shoe, the light grey bracket, and is clamped there like a finder. The base runs along almost the whole rail, so the rail rests on it over its full length instead of only at the shoe; that keeps a long rail from bending or rocking.
  • The rail (2) is screwed onto the base with T-nuts in the base’s slot. Its slots leave room in front of and behind the holder for more, for example a red light.
  • The camera mount (3) clamps into one of the rail’s slots with its screw. Note which slot it is: put back into the same one, the eyepiece point still fits the next night.
  • The phone holder (4) has a swivel joint and a knurled locking ring. The phone goes onto the round plate with its back to it, so its back camera looks to the right, towards the opening of the tube. Tilt the joint until the phone is roughly parallel to the tube, then lock the ring; Zenigo takes care of the rest when you set the eyepiece point.

Finder shoe adapter

The foot for Explore Scientific copies the original ES adapter, including its stop notch. The V-block has channels across it for cable ties or hose clamps; they close towards the V with 45° slopes, so they print without supports too. The base carries a slot for the T-nuts of the rail, sized for 20-series “slot 6” nuts; if yours differ, adjust it under T-slot. The cavity closes with two 45° slopes, so it prints without supports.
