This page explains every part of Zenigo. New here? Getting started walks you through the first night step by step.

Using the app

The screen

  1. Directions: how far to turn and tilt the telescope
  2. Menu
  3. Crosshair: where the telescope points, with the finder and eyepiece rings
  4. Target (red ring), with the arrow from the crosshair
  5. Buttons, here with their labels
  6. Target pill: how far away it is, ✕ clears it
  7. Status and the source of the view
  8. Locate

The green circles are the stars the last Locate recognised; the dark shapes at the bottom are trees in the camera image.

Directions at the top
How many degrees to turn the telescope left or right (azimuth) and to tilt it up or down (altitude) to reach the target. Where the telescope points: without a target the two boxes show the telescope’s own azimuth (with the compass direction) and altitude, whenever the map follows it (Align, compass or after a Locate), handy for directions like “at azimuth 45°, 30° high”. With a target, tap the boxes to switch between the way to it and where the telescope points; the app remembers the choice.
Target (bottom left)
The current target as a pill with its state: how far away it is, “in the eyepiece” (green) or “below the horizon”; before the first locate, how high it stands and in which direction. Tap it to open the target panel; its ✕ clears the target, so the red ring, the arrow and the directions disappear. A “Next” button above it jumps to the next object on your plan.
Map or image
The camera image or the star map, with stars (fainter ones as you zoom in), constellation lines and names (both can be switched in Settings → General → Display), Messier and other deep-sky objects, planets, satellites when online, and the horizon with the cardinal points. The orange crosshair is where the telescope points; map and image are shifted so that it always stays in the middle of the screen (on tablets and computers in the middle of the part not covered by open panels), even when the phone sits crooked on the tube, the red ring is the target and the red arrow shows the way to it. Green circles mark the stars recognised in the last image. Optional Telrad and eyepiece rings sit around the crosshair, and small marks show objects you planned (amber flag), starred (star outline) or have seen (green tick).
Menu (top left)
The button with the Z logo (top left) opens the menu: logbook, label a night photo, time, settings, sync with other devices (when the server offers it), live connection, Zenigo Pro, Tonight for your location and links to this help. Links marked with an arrow open another website in a new tab. On phones, menus and panels slide up from the bottom and can be swiped down to close. On tablets and computers the menu, the logbook (with its entries and “Seen”) and the settings open on the left, everything about the sky and objects (details, target, search, list, filter, quick settings) on the right; they float over the map, the buttons move next to them, and the map glides along so that its middle, the crosshair and an object shown with Show on map stay in the middle of the free part. From 1000 pixels width the menu and a panel on the right can be open at the same time. A panel on the right stays open while you work in it: setting a target shows the target panel, Seen returns to the details or the target, and tapping the map shows the next object’s details. Close it with ✕ or the Esc key. Panels opened from another one (details from the list, the search or Tonight; an entry from the logbook) have a back arrow: it goes back there, ✕ closes them altogether.
Hiding the pills bottom left
The small ‹ above the pills at the bottom left (target, corrections, filter, status) pushes them out to the left, for a freer view of the map. A small tab at the left edge brings them back; a red dot on it means a target is set. The app remembers it. Messages below the menu button close with their ✕ or go by themselves. Settings → General → Display → Info messages turns off the ones that only confirm something (a mode switched, “Compass mode: rough alignment …”, the switch of the direction boxes); warnings, errors and steps you have to do are always shown.
Status (bottom left)
What the app is doing or has done, for example “Solved · 42 stars · 0.8 s”, and below it the source: live camera, photo or sky map, and whether it follows the compass or the motion sensor. Active extras appear here too: a compass correction, a filter or another time. Their ✕ switches them off. Above them the round eyepiece view appears when it is switched on.
Gestures
Pinch, double-tap or the mouse wheel to zoom (the map goes from 140° across down to half a degree, close enough to separate Jupiter’s moons), drag to pan or, on the free sky map and around a solved photo, to look around. Tap a star, planet, deep-sky object or satellite for its details. On the free map you can look all the way up to the zenith and down to the point below your feet. With a mouse the pointer is a normal arrow, a closed hand while you drag, and a crosshair while you set the eyepiece point.

Buttons

The round buttons on the right. A dimmed button can’t be used right now; tap it to see why.

Quick settings
The settings you change at the telescope, from top to bottom:
  • Eyepiece: the eyepiece or finder you are using, with the Eyepiece view switch below it.
  • Eyepiece point with Reset, and Compass correction with Remove.
  • Red light.
  • Solve a photo: Night photo or From gallery (opens it labelled).
  • Camera, with the Mirrored image switch.
  • Pointing: Camera or Top edge.
  • On the map: constellation lines, constellation names, constellation figures, star names, object names, list marks and Position numbers: a small box at the top right, below the target line (off by default). It shows one short line, the field of view and, after a locate, its accuracy and how many stars matched; tap it for the full list: your location and where it comes from (GPS with its accuracy and time, entered by hand, or still the default), the field of view, and after a locate its accuracy, the matched stars and where the telescope points (RA/Dec, altitude, azimuth).
  • Button labels.
  • Location in one line, with GPS and an Edit button that shows the latitude and longitude fields.
All settings at the bottom opens the full settings.
Target
The current target at a glance: how far to turn and tilt, how high it is now, when it is best placed tonight, whether it is planned, starred or seen, with its details (brightness, size or distance, coordinates, your history) and links, and buttons for Seen, Next, Plan, change and clear. Red while a target is set; without one it opens Find target. Tapping the target line at the top of the map opens it too.
Sound
Appears when a target is set: guidance you can hear while you push, without looking at the screen. It works once the position is known (Align or after a locate) and guides one axis after the other, like you push a Dobson:
  • Turn first: clicks. A high “tick” means turn right, a low “tock” turn left.
  • Then tilt: once the direction is right within about a degree, chirps. Rising means tilt up, falling tilt down.
  • The closer you get, the faster they repeat. When the target is in the eyepiece field a soft chord sounds, then it is quiet until the target drifts out again.
Tap the button again to switch it off. While the sound is on, the screen stays on; on an iPhone it also plays with the silent switch on, where iOS allows it.
List
Your observing list: planned for tonight, seen tonight and starred; the number shows how many planned objects are still to do.
Find target
Choose what you want to look at: a list of what is well placed now (higher than 15° and within your telescope’s reach), categories, or search by name, Messier number or star name; the search field is ready to type as soon as it opens. See Categories. A result in the list opens its details (with tonight’s bar), where Set as target makes it the target and ← goes back to the search. The current target is also shown as a pill at the bottom left: tap it for the target panel, ✕ clears the target.
Filter
Choose what the map shows. Lights up while a filter is active. See Filter.
Eyepiece point
Tell the app where the telescope points relative to the phone. Available after a locate, or with Align on the map. See Eyepiece point.
Continuous
Locates again and again by itself while the camera runs. After the first solve each further one only checks the predicted position, which takes a few milliseconds. Needs the live camera.
Align
Lets the map and the directions follow the phone’s movement. Switching it off leaves the map where it was, with the same field, so you can look around from there; leaving the camera does the same. See Sky map, camera and Align. On iPhone the first tap asks for permission to use the motion sensors.
Camera
While the live camera runs, a tap switches it off (you see the sky map). Otherwise it opens a small menu: Live camera; Night photo with your phone’s camera app; or Photo from the gallery, which opens a photo labelled. A tap anywhere else closes the menu.
Locate
The large orange button. Starts the camera if needed, captures a few frames and works out exactly where the camera points (plate solving). After a night photo it solves that photo. If a locate takes longer (for example while the field of view of a new camera or photo is searched), the button turns into Stop with a spinning ring; tap it to cancel. Stopping also ends Continuous mode.

Sky map, camera and Align

Zenigo knows four ways of working out where you are looking. The status at the bottom left always shows which one is active.

Free sky map
Without camera and without Align you see the sky above your location as a map. Drag to look around, pinch to zoom. Handy for planning.
Compass
With Align on but before any locate, the map follows the phone using compass and motion sensor. Good for rough orientation, for example at dusk or through clouds, but often 10° or more off because the telescope’s metal disturbs the compass. A compass correction improves this.
Located
After a successful Locate the position comes from the stars themselves and is accurate to a fraction of a degree.
Motion sensor
With Align on after a locate, the gyroscope carries the exact position along while you push. It stays accurate for minutes; tap Locate again now and then, or use Continuous.

Pointing more than about 50° up, the compass is unreliable, so the app keeps its last good value and relies on the gyroscope. Near the zenith a Dobson needs large azimuth turns for small movements on the sky; the short route takes this into account.

How locating works

Locate averages several camera frames, finds the points of light and compares their pattern with a catalogue of about 5,000 stars down to magnitude 6. That works anywhere in the sky and doesn’t need the compass or the location; the location is only used to convert the result into directions for your telescope.

  • The first locate also measures the camera’s field of view and saves it. It takes up to about ten seconds, later ones about a second.
  • It needs around ten stars in the image. Fields narrower than about 10° can’t be solved, so it does not work through the finder or the eyepiece.
  • Trees, houses and clouds are ignored as far as possible, but the more open sky, the better.
  • Night photo: a browser only gets a video stream from the camera, with at most about 1/30 second per frame. Your phone’s camera app can expose for several seconds in its night mode and sees far more stars. Camera button → Night photo (or Quick settings → Solve a photo → Night photo) opens the camera app: take the photo with the phone in its holder, without moving the telescope, and Zenigo solves it right away. If Align is on, the map then follows the telescope from that photo, as precisely as after a live Locate, and the photo stays pinned to its stars; otherwise turning Align on does that. Whether the camera app offers its night mode here depends on the phone; through a mirror the photo is flipped back like the live image.
  • Gallery photos open labelled, in a view of their own. Their field of view is searched every time.

Labelled night photos

Menu → Label a night photo (or Camera button → Photo from the gallery) opens a sky photo you took: Zenigo finds its stars and draws on it what is there. The photo fills the screen; pinch, double-tap or scroll to zoom, drag to move it. Tap a name for the object’s details.

At the top
‹ closes the photo and goes back to the sky map. The title names the constellations the photo shows most of, below it the date and time it was taken. ⋯ offers Show on the sky map, Another photo, Solve again and When it was taken. On the sky map the photo stays pinned to its stars, also with Align on: a chip at the bottom left (“Photo:” and its name) turns the map to it, or says which way to turn the phone; its ✕ removes the photo.
When it was taken
Stars stay where they are, but the planets and the Moon move: to put them in the right place, Zenigo reads the time (and, if it is there, the place) from the photo’s EXIF data. The pill below the title says where the time came from. Without a time in the photo it asks for it; tap it to set date and time (the same panel as Time). Until then planets and the Moon are left out. If the photo gives no time zone, the camera’s clock is taken as your time zone. Leaving the photo brings back the time shown before.
Layers
Three styles: Clean (names only), Labelled (constellation lines and names) and Atlas (also the constellation figures and a grid of right ascension and declination). Below them each layer on its own: constellation lines, names and figures, star names, galaxies, nebulae and clusters (outlined at their true size, the larger galaxies as ellipses at their angle), planets and the Moon, the grid, and the stars the photo was solved with. How many labels goes from the brightest objects to everything in the catalogue: first Messier objects and popular showpieces, then NGC and IC objects, other catalogues only with many labels.
In this photo
Everything labelled, by kind (deep sky, planets, stars, constellations), with a small cut-out of your photo for each. “new” marks objects that are not in your logbook yet. Tap one to zoom to it on the photo and open its details. Show more lists the fainter ones too. Star the new ones puts them on your wish list; Log as seen enters them in the logbook for the night of the photo, with the note “On a photo”.
Save · Share
Makes a new image: your photo with the labels as you see them, and below it a caption with a title, date and time, field of view and centre. The place (rounded to about 10 km) is only added if you switch it on: the one in the photo, or, when the photo has none (photos picked from the gallery often come without it), your location set in Zenigo. The Zenigo logo (with “Pro” while Zenigo Pro runs) and “zenigo.app” sit at the right of the caption, or without a caption in a small pill in the corner. Sizes: Full (the photo’s size; very large photos are reduced to what the browser can draw, about 16 megapixels), Large (2048 pixels on the long side) or For posting (1080). Save image downloads it as a JPEG, Share hands it to another app where the device offers that. Your original stays as it is, and the new image carries no GPS or other EXIF data.

Everything happens on the device: the photo isn’t uploaded. It needs about ten stars and a field of at least about 10°, like Locate. Photos through a telescope are usually too narrow.

At the telescope, step by step

Locate (plate solving) and the eyepiece point answer two different questions. Locate finds out where the phone’s camera looks: it detects the stars in the camera image and matches them against the catalogue, independent of the compass and accurate to a fraction of a degree. The eyepiece point stores where the telescope looks compared with the camera, because the phone never sits exactly parallel to the tube. Align keeps both up to date while you push the telescope, using the phone’s motion sensors.

  1. Mount the phone firmly, its back camera looking along the tube towards the opening. Turn on the phone’s rotation lock.
  2. Turn on Align (compass button) and leave it on. Until the first Locate the map follows the compass, often 10° or more off.
  3. Centre a bright star in your lowest-power eyepiece by hand. Use a star, not a planet or a nebula: it is a sharp point.
  4. Tap Locate without touching the telescope and wait for “Solved”. If the compass was far off, Zenigo asks whether to correct it: tap Correct.
  5. Tap Eyepiece point, then tap that star on the screen. It snaps to the nearest star and names it. From now on the orange crosshair marks where the telescope points.
  6. Check it on a second bright star elsewhere: centre it in the eyepiece and tap Locate. The crosshair should sit on it. If not, set the eyepiece point again.
  7. Choose a target and push. The directions follow the telescope live. The motion sensor drifts a little over long moves: close to the target, tap Locate again (or turn on Continuous) before you look through the eyepiece.

Setup assistant: tapping Locate before Align is on or before an eyepiece point was ever set opens a small card at the bottom of the screen (the middle with the crosshair stays free) that goes through these steps and switches things on by itself: Align and the camera (with the permission prompts), a bright star or planet that is easy to reach with a Dobson (15–60° high (within your telescope’s reach), best around 35°; up to four in different directions to choose from, any other bright star or planet works too; not the Moon, whose glare blinds the camera) with your lowest-power eyepiece, Locate, the compass correction (applied without asking), the eyepiece point (the card shrinks to one line: “Tap Vega on the screen”), and an optional check on a second star at least 30° away, which reports how far off the crosshair is. On later nights, with the eyepiece point already set, it is short: sensors, Locate on a star, “Does the crosshair sit on it?”. Just locate skips it for the rest of the session; the menu → Setup assistant starts it any time. Without a camera it sets the eyepiece point on the map instead.

The eyepiece point stays saved as long as the holder doesn’t move, also on later nights; then steps 3–6 are only needed when the crosshair no longer fits. Without detected stars there is a rougher way on the map, see below.

Eyepiece point

The phone never sits exactly parallel to the tube. The eyepiece point stores the offset between camera and telescope, so the crosshair and the directions refer to the telescope.

  1. Centre a bright star in a low-power eyepiece by hand.
  2. Tap Locate.
  3. Tap Eyepiece point, then the same star on the screen. The tap snaps to the nearest star and confirms it by name.

Without image detection (camera off, or top edge mode): turn on Align, centre a bright star in the eyepiece, tap Eyepiece point and then tap that star on the map. The app corrects the compass by the azimuth difference and stores the height difference between phone and telescope. The crosshair then marks where the telescope points, accurate to about a degree near that star; repeat it on another star when you move far across the sky. The pill at the bottom left then reads “Aligned on the map”; its ✕ removes both the compass correction and the height difference, so the eyepiece point is back as before. If the star you tap stands more than 15° higher or lower than the phone points, only the compass is corrected: that much means another star or a phone not on the tube. An eyepiece point more than 5° from the image centre is always shown at the bottom left (“Eyepiece point 40° off centre”), because it shifts the whole view, the horizon too; its ✕ sets it back to the image centre.

Tap the button again to cancel. Camera and top edge mode each keep their own eyepiece point. Settings → Camera & sensors → Eyepiece point shows the stored offset, for example “2.8° right and 13.9° above the image centre”, and can reset it to the image centre; Quick settings has the same Reset. Set it again whenever the holder has moved.

Compass correction

The phone’s compass is often several degrees off, especially near the metal of a telescope. After a locate with Align on, Zenigo compares the compass with the exact direction from the stars. If they differ by more than about 1.5°, it asks: “The compass is −12.3° off. Correct it?”

  • Correct saves the measured value and uses it in compass mode from now on.
  • ✕ dismisses the question. It only comes back if the measured error changes by more than 3°.
  • While a correction is active, “Compass corrected −12.3°” is shown at the bottom left. Its ✕ removes the correction, and the compass is used as it is again. Quick settings → Compass correction → Remove and Settings → Camera & sensors → Compass do the same.

The correction only matters before the first locate. After a locate the position comes from the stars.

Aligning on a star by hand: in compass mode, aim the telescope (or the top of the phone) at a bright star or planet, tap it on the map and choose I’m pointing at it. The app sets the compass correction so that the direction matches. This works without camera and is the best way to make top edge mode accurate. If the altitude differs a lot as well, the location is probably wrong.

Without camera: top edge

If you don’t want to use the camera, for example with the phone strapped flat along the tube or held in the hand like a pointer, choose Settings → Camera & sensors → Pointing → Top edge (or Quick settings → Pointing → Top edge). The top edge of the phone then counts as the pointing direction, and the map shows what lies in that direction.

The map always stays upright, like in a planetarium program: up on the screen is towards the zenith, down towards the horizon, however you hold the phone, whether you point up or down, with the screen facing up or towards you, or with the phone strapped to the side of the tube. Moving the telescope right moves the sky to the left, as in the eyepiece of a finder. Pointing straight at the zenith, “up” is undefined; the map then keeps its last orientation.

  • Align switches on automatically; the position comes from compass and motion sensor only.
  • The camera can be used in this mode too, if a periscope mirror or prism turns its view over the top of the phone.
    • When you start the camera, the app asks whether the image is mirrored. Choose Mirrored image on for a single mirror or a simple 90° prism, Without mirroring for optics that keep the image the right way round (two mirrors, a roof prism). The answer is remembered for the camera; change it any time with Mirrored image in Quick settings.
    • Locate, Continuous and the eyepiece point then work as usual. The first Locate with Align on teaches the app where the camera looks.
    • The image is flipped so that the screen side of the phone is up: with the phone on top of the tube, the sky is upright in the image as well. While the camera image is shown, the view turns with the phone (it has to match the image); without it, the map is upright again.
  • The compass is often several degrees off, and the phone is rarely exactly parallel to the tube. Centre a bright star in the eyepiece and set the eyepiece point on it: that corrects both at once, and the crosshair then marks where the telescope points.
  • Expect an accuracy of one to a few degrees: enough with a finder or a low-power eyepiece, not for small objects at high magnification.

Telrad rings and eyepiece view

Enter your telescope and eyepieces once in Settings → Equipment (with several telescopes or binoculars, see Instruments): the focal length of the telescope, and for each eyepiece its focal length and apparent field (printed on it or in its specs, about 50° for a Plössl). The app works out the magnification and the true field, for example 25 mm in a 1200 mm telescope: 48× and 1.1°. Choose the eyepiece you are using in Quick settings.

Rings on the map
Around the crosshair the map can show the Telrad rings (red, 0.5°, 2° and 4°, as in many star charts), the true field of the eyepiece (white) and of the finder (dashed). So you see before looking whether the target will be in the field.
Eyepiece view Pro
A round view at the bottom left shows what you should see in the eyepiece: stars to magnitude 9 (with the extended catalogue to 11, as far as your aperture shows), Messier and other deep-sky objects at their true size, the Moon and planets, and your target, or a red arrow at the edge pointing towards it. Tap it (or its label) to switch between eyepiece and finder: a finder scope shows its field, a red-dot finder or a Telrad the sky as with the naked eye with the dot or the rings; the gear opens the telescope, eyepiece and finder settings; ✕ hides it. Switch it on in Quick settings or in Settings → Equipment.
Orientation
A Newtonian such as a Dobson shows the sky upside down, a star diagonal on a refractor shows it mirrored. Choose this in Settings → Equipment → Eyepiece view orientation. The finder has its own Finder image: upside down for a straight finder, upright for a right-angle correct-image finder, mirrored for a right-angle finder that doesn’t correct the image. How far a Newtonian turns the view also depends on where the focuser sits and how you look in: compare the view with the eyepiece once and turn it with Turn the view by until the star patterns match.

The top line says “… should be in the eyepiece” when the target is within 70 % of the radius of the selected eyepiece’s true field: for the 25 mm eyepiece above, within about 0.4°. With no eyepiece entered, it appears within 0.4°.

Barlow lens

A Barlow lens sits between the focuser and the eyepiece and multiplies the magnification of every eyepiece, usually by 2×; the true field gets smaller by the same factor. Choose yours under Settings → Equipment → Barlow lens. Each eyepiece then also appears with the Barlow, for example “10 mm + 2× · 240×”: in Quick settings, on the map rings, in the eyepiece view, in the logbook and in the eyepiece advice, which takes the eyepiece alone when it is about as good.

Best eyepiece Pro

The details of a deep-sky object, the Moon or a planet, and the target panel, say which of your eyepieces suits it best and why, for example “10 mm · 120× · It fills about half the field; exit pupil 1.7 mm”. Below it all your eyepieces are listed with a short verdict such as “looks tiny”, “frames it well” or “larger than the field”. Tap one to see above what it does for this object; Use it then makes it the eyepiece in use, for the map rings and the eyepiece view.

  • Galaxies and nebulae: the object should fit and fill a good part of the field, at an exit pupil of about 2–4 mm, which shows faint light best.
  • Globular clusters and small planetary nebulae: more magnification, an exit pupil of about 1–2 mm, to resolve stars or make a small disc bigger.
  • Open clusters: mostly framing, with some sky around the cluster.
  • Moon: the highest magnification that still shows the whole Moon, plus a tip for details along the line between day and night.
  • Planets: magnification relative to the aperture, at most about 150–280× depending on the planet, as the air rarely allows more. It says how big the planet then looks compared with the full Moon to the naked eye.

When your widest eyepiece is a different one, it says to find the object with that first and then switch. The exit pupil is eyepiece focal length ÷ focal ratio, so the app needs the aperture (mirror diameter, 200 mm for an 8-inch Dobson). Enter it in Settings → Equipment. Without the aperture or without eyepieces, the details show a note with a link to these settings instead. The settings then show the focal ratio, the highest useful magnification (about twice the aperture in mm) and each eyepiece’s exit pupil.

Planning

Categories in Find target

The chips under the search field are categories. Tap one or more; they stay selected for next time, and typing narrows the list down further.

My telescope
Objects your telescope shows well: bright enough for its aperture (for galaxies and nebulae the brightness spread over their area counts, which is what makes a large faint galaxy hard to see) and not far larger than your widest eyepiece’s field: those are better in binoculars. Its details name the eyepiece that frames it best. Needs the aperture in Settings → Equipment; the sky is assumed to be dark.
In reach
Objects that come into your telescope’s reach from your location at some time of night or year: they climb at least as high as its lowest altitude and don’t always stay above its highest. Without a set range, all objects that rise at your location at all. It doesn’t depend on the time; for what you can reach right now use Good now.
Good now
Objects well placed at the shown time: at least 25° high (planets 15°), within your telescope’s reach and still high in an hour, the sky dark enough, and galaxies and nebulae not next to a bright Moon. With another time set, it reads “Good at …”.
Popular
A curated list of about 60 showpieces for small and medium telescopes from mid-northern latitudes: the Moon and bright planets, the Pleiades, the Double Cluster, M13, the Ring, Dumbbell and Orion Nebulae, the Andromeda, Whirlpool and Sombrero Galaxies, the Needle Galaxy and more.
Messier, Caldwell, Herschel 400
The classic observing lists: Messier’s 110 objects; Patrick Moore’s Caldwell list of 109 more for amateur telescopes; and the Herschel 400, 400 of William Herschel’s objects chosen for telescopes from about 15 cm.
Solar system, Galaxies, Clusters, Nebulae
The kind of object.

Only Good now depends on the time: the other categories list objects whether they are up or not, and the altitude on the right says where each one is now (“too low” or “too high” when it is out of your telescope’s reach).

Combining: My telescope, In reach, Good now and Popular all have to apply (My telescope and In reach together: everything your telescope shows well and can point at from here), so together they show the popular objects that suit your telescope and are well placed now. Among the lists and among the kinds one is enough: Messier and Caldwell show both lists; add Galaxies to see only their galaxies. The best matches come first.

Why an object suits you or not is in its details, under Suits you?: the lists it is on, how bright it is for your telescope and which eyepiece frames it, and where it stands at the shown time.

Targets and object details

Targets are the Sun, the Moon, the planets, all 110 Messier objects, about 1,900 further deep-sky objects (NGC, IC and other catalogues, for example “NGC 869” or “North America Nebula”) and the bright named stars. The Target button shows the current target in a panel: directions from the crosshair, its altitude and direction now, tonight’s visibility and what you can do with it. The Moon is drawn on the map in its current phase, at true size when zoomed in, with the lit side towards the Sun; planets get their true size too, and zoomed in on Jupiter you see its four big moons.

Tap any object on the map, or its name, for its details. The buttons at the bottom of the details (Seen, Set as target, Plan, Show on map) always stay at the bottom; links to SEDS, Wikipedia and others sit just above them. The target panel works the same way, with Seen, Plan, Change, Clear and Next on your plan; so do the observing list and the “Seen” entry: only their content scrolls. The details show brightness, distance (Moon and planets), altitude and azimuth right now, coordinates and the distance from the eyepiece. Every star drawn on the map can be tapped, also the faint ones down to 9 mag that appear when you zoom in; those have no names and show as “Star · 7.8 mag” with a SIMBAD link for their position. Show on map (while neither Align nor the live camera is on) turns the map to the object, puts it in the middle with a white ring and zooms to 20–40° across, so you still see the constellation around it; on a phone the details close for it. Set as target makes it your target; on the current target the button reads Target ✓; tapping it again removes the target. “Clear target” in Find target does the same.

The buttons at the bottom open good sources in a new tab:

  • Messier objects: SEDS Messier Database (data, photos, observing notes), In-The-Sky (visibility and finder charts), Wikipedia
  • Sun, Moon and planets: NASA Science, Wikipedia
  • Stars: Wikipedia and SIMBAD (the professional star database)

Moon and planets in detail

The details of the Moon and the planets (and the target panel when one of them is your target) show more, calculated on the phone without internet:

  • Moon: the phase as a picture, how much is lit, its age, the next full and new Moon, the libration (which edge is tilted towards you, so you see a little further around it) and which well-known craters and mountains lie near the terminator right now, where shadows make them stand out.
  • All planets: the size of the disc in arcseconds and how big it looks in your selected eyepiece compared with the full Moon to the naked eye, the distance from the Sun in the sky (morning or evening sky), how long its light takes to reach us, and the next opposition or, for Mercury and Venus, the next greatest elongation.
  • Mercury, Venus and Mars: the phase as a picture, turned like in your eyepiece.
  • Saturn: how far the rings are tilted towards us and which side we see.
  • Jupiter: a picture of its four big moons Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto, turned like in your eyepiece (setting “Eyepiece view orientation”), and when a moon or its shadow crosses Jupiter in the next 24 hours, with a note if Jupiter isn’t up then.

Observing the Sun

Never look at the Sun through a telescope or finder without a certified solar filter covering the front of the telescope. Focused sunlight blinds instantly and permanently, a single glance is enough.

Choosing the Sun as a target first asks you to confirm that a certified full-aperture filter (ISO 12312-2 or solar film) sits on the front of the telescope, that the finder is covered or removed and that children are supervised. Locate doesn’t work in daylight, so guidance to the Sun relies on compass and motion sensor. To aim without looking, turn the tube until its shadow is as small and round as possible.

Extended catalogue Pro

With Zenigo Pro, the whole NGC and IC catalogues come to the map, the search and the eyepiece view: about 10,000 more galaxies, nebulae and clusters, down to magnitude 15 (from OpenNGC), and about 790,000 more stars down to magnitude 11 (Tycho-2 and Hipparcos via AT-HYG). Useful for galaxy groups like Stephan’s Quintet next to NGC 7331, and for hopping from star to star at higher magnification.

Logbook

Seen in an object’s details logs it: date and time, the instrument (the one in use, another of your instruments, binoculars or the naked eye; instruments of earlier entries can be chosen too), with a telescope its eyepiece and magnification, altitude, Moon and site are filled in. Choose easy, hard or not found, optionally seeing and transparency (1–5, kept for the rest of the night) and a note, then Save or Save, next to go straight to the next planned object.

The logbook (Menu → Logbook; ← goes back to the menu) shows your progress (Messier objects, planets, nights) and lists the entries by night, newest first.

  • Suggestions: when Messier objects you haven’t seen are well placed tonight, a line says how many. Its Plan button adds up to 12 of them to tonight’s plan, the brightest first.
  • Open an entry: tap it to see or change it. The arrows step to the newer and older entries, ← (or ✕) goes back to the logbook where you were; changes are kept. ⓘ opens the object’s details (on tablets and computers on the right, next to the entry).
  • Delete an entry: open it and tap Delete, then the red Yes, delete that takes its place (Cancel keeps it). A deleted entry can’t be restored.
  • One object only: All entries in the target panel, or All entries for … in an object’s details, opens the logbook with just that object. The chip “… only ✕” at the top shows all entries again.

Object details show how often and when you last saw an object.

Logbook statistics Pro

The logbook’s Statistics tab. Without Pro it shows the overview; with Zenigo Pro all of it, for All time, the Last 12 months or one year:

Export
The arrow at the top of the logbook: all entries or just the filtered ones, as a spreadsheet (CSV) (free), as OpenAstronomyLog (OAL 2.1, the exchange format of many observing programs) with your sites, one session per night, the objects, instruments and eyepieces Pro, or a Year in review, an image of a year to save or share Pro. Tonight

The Tonight section of the menu starts with Darkness, Moon and what’s up, calculated on the phone for your location, also offline:

Reading the night bar

The bar under each object in the list, in Find target, in its details and in the target panel is tonight from sunset (left, with its time) to sunrise (right):

18:23
07:46
  • Amber: the object is above the horizon, but low (under 20°), out of your telescope’s reach or the sky is still too bright.
  • Green: well placed: higher than 20°, within your telescope’s reach and the sky dark enough (the Sun at least 12° below the horizon; for planets 6° is enough).
  • Grey: below the horizon.
  • Red mark: now.

In the example the object is well placed from evening until about midnight, then sinks low and sets in the early morning. The text above the bar says the same in words, for example “best 21:40, south · low after 00:30”. In the details and the target panel the bar sits in a Tonight box, with when the object is well placed and when it rises, culminates and sets during the night.

In the details and the target panel the bar is larger: under it are the times when the green part starts and ends, and a small legend explains the colours. If you set how low and how high your telescope points, dotted marks above the bar show when the object comes into reach (in) and when it leaves it (out), for example “in 22:40” when it sinks below your highest altitude. When it stays in reach the whole night, there are no marks.

Time: the sky later

To plan, look at the sky at another time: Menu → Time. Drag the slider (12 hours back to 24 hours ahead), use the buttons Now, ±1 h, Dark tonight, Midnight and End of darkness, or pick any date and time. The map, the directions at the top, the target search (“well placed”) and the object details then show the sky at that time, and the time keeps running from there.

While another time is shown, it appears at the bottom left; tap it to change it, ✕ returns to now. With Align on, the map shows what will be in the direction of the phone at that time. Locate, the eyepiece point and aligning on a star need the sky as it is now, so they switch back to the current time.

ISS and satellites Pro

Switched on in the Filter (they are off at first), the map shows the ISS, the Chinese space station Tiangong and about 150 other bright satellites. Bright cyan dots are lit by the Sun and can be seen, with a short tail showing where they came from; grey rings are in Earth’s shadow and invisible. The ISS is always labelled, other satellites when you zoom in. Tap one for its height, distance and a link to Heavens-Above, or make it your target: the directions at the top then follow it.

A satellite you choose as target, whose details are open or that you jumped to with Show on map is always drawn, also below the horizon (dimmed, “below the horizon”) and with satellites switched off in the filter. While it is below the horizon, the target pill at the bottom left gives the time of the next pass, and the target panel says when it next comes up (within 24 hours), from which direction, how high it gets and whether you can see it then: only when it is lit by the Sun while your sky is dark. The ISS passes over a place in groups, so the next pass can be many hours away.

Orbits change quickly, so the app loads them fresh from the internet (from CelesTrak, through our server) and doesn’t keep them: offline, satellites are not shown. Even while they are switched off, they can be found in the search and chosen as a target. With the time set to later, the map shows where they will be, for example to catch the next ISS pass. Satellites move up to about a degree per second, too fast to follow by pushing a Dobson; it is easier to wait at a point of their path.

General

How the image and the map look:

Equipment

At the top, Your instruments: the telescope or binoculars you observe with, marked “in use”. Below, the settings of the one you tapped (the one in use, unless you tapped another). See Instruments.

A telescope
Its optics, used for the map rings, the eyepiece view and the best eyepiece. See Telrad rings and eyepiece view.
  • Name (as the logbook notes it) and the kind: Dobsonian, refractor, Newtonian, Maksutov or SCT, or another telescope.
  • Mount: alt-azimuth like a Dobson, or equatorial; used for the short route in the planned list.
  • Focal length and Aperture of the telescope.
  • Lowest it points and Highest it points: how far down and up you can point the telescope, for example 15° because of the rocker box, a hedge or the house, or 80° because the tube hits the base (0° and 90° = the whole sky). Zenigo then keeps to this range: Find target (well placed now, Good now), Tonight, the best times and the green part of the night bars, the planned list, Next, the suggestions in the logbook and the stars of the setup assistant. A target out of reach is marked in the target line (“75° high: too high for your telescope”), and the map shows the two limits as dashed circles.
  • Finder: red-dot or LED finder, Telrad, a 6×30, 8×50 or 9×50 finder scope (straight or right-angle), or another one. A finder scope’s field of view is set from its type (about 7°, 6° or 5.5°), for another one you enter it: the apparent field of its eyepiece ÷ its magnification, for example 50° ÷ 8 = 6.3°. A red-dot finder and the Telrad don’t magnify: the map shows the red dot (or the Telrad’s rings) where the telescope points, and the eyepiece view shows the sky as with the naked eye with the dot or the rings. Choosing the Telrad turns its rings on, choosing another finder turns them off again. Finder image sets how the eyepiece view shows the finder.
  • Focuser: 1.25″, 2″ (takes 1.25″ eyepieces with its adapter) or 0.965″.
  • Your eyepieces, each with focal length, apparent field and barrel (1.25″, 2″ or 0.965″); Add eyepiece adds one. An eyepiece too large for the focuser, a 2″ one in a 1.25″ focuser, is marked “Doesn’t fit”.
  • Barlow lens: none, or its factor (1.5× to 5×); each eyepiece then also counts with it. See Barlow lens.
  • The rings on the map and the eyepiece view, with its orientation and Turn the view by.
  • Replace with a new instrument (with one instrument) or Delete this instrument (with several).
Binoculars
Name, Magnification, Aperture and Field (printed on them, often as metres at 1000 m: 105 m is about 6°), and How you use them: hand-held, or on a mount with the phone attached for push-to. Their field is the ring on the map and the eyepiece view, and the details of an object say how it fits in it. They reach the whole sky and have no finder.

Instruments Pro

Each telescope keeps its own eyepieces, Barlow lens, finder, reach, eyepiece view orientation and eyepiece point (on each device, since it depends on how the phone sits on it), and remembers the eyepiece you used last. Binoculars are an instrument of their own. Without Pro you have one instrument with as many eyepieces as you like: change it, or replace it with a new one. With Zenigo Pro you keep several.

Adding one
Settings › Equipment › Add an instrument (or + Add in Quick settings): first the kind, then its data, then its eyepieces. A common size (8″ f/6, 80/600, 10×50 …) fills in the name, focal length and aperture; the finder and mount start as usual for the kind. The usual 25 mm and 10 mm 1.25″ eyepieces are there already; tap to add others (with a 2″ focuser also 2″ ones), take them over from your other telescopes (those that fit its focuser), or type one in with its barrel. Add and use switches to it, Add only keeps the one you have in use.
Switching
In Quick settings (the instruments at the top), with the instrument under the eyepiece view (it opens a list of your instruments and the eyepieces of the one in use), or with Use in Settings › Equipment. Zenigo never switches by itself. After switching, the map rings, the eyepiece view, the eyepiece advice, My telescope in Find target, the reach and the short route follow the instrument in use.
Changing another one
Tap it in Settings › Equipment: its settings show below, marked “Not in use on this device”, and changes go to it. Use it switches to it.
With sync
Your instruments are the same on all your devices; which one a device uses, and its eyepiece point, stay with that device.

Camera & sensors

Pointing
Whether the back camera looks along the telescope (default, needed for Locate) or the top edge of the phone points along it.
Eyepiece point
Shows the stored eyepiece point; Reset to image centre clears it.
Camera
Which camera Locate uses. “Back camera (automatic)” is the phone’s main camera. The list also shows other cameras of the phone and any connected USB webcam or HDMI capture device; the names appear after the camera has been started once. Field of view and eyepiece point are remembered separately for each camera. With an external camera, Align is off, because the phone’s motion sensors don’t know how that camera moves; use Locate or Continuous instead.
Mirrored image
Switch this on when the camera looks through a mirror or prism, for example a 90° mirror so the phone can lie flat on the tube. A mirror turns the image into its mirror image, and a mirrored star pattern can’t be matched with the catalogue.
  • With the option on, the app flips the image back before locating and showing it: left to right in camera mode, top to bottom in top edge mode.
  • It works with both pointing modes: in camera mode for a mirror that sends the view anywhere, in top edge mode for a periscope over the top of the phone, where the app asks about it when you first start the camera.
  • It is remembered per camera, and is also in Quick settings.
  • Align keeps working. With Align on, the first Locate also teaches the app in which direction the mirror sends the view (it recognises any 90° arrangement). From then on the map follows the phone as usual.
Camera field of view
The angle the camera covers along the long side of the image, measured on the first successful locate (typically 60–75° for main cameras). Measure again if you switch camera or zoom. The search range limits where the measurement looks.
Capture
How Locate takes its image:
  • Frames to stack (1–90, 24 by default): how many video frames are averaged per locate. Every four times as many frames halve the noise, so fainter stars appear: 24 frames take about a second, 60 frames two to four seconds. Continuous locating uses a third of the frames.
  • While stacking, the motion sensor watches the telescope with a tolerance of about two pixels. Frames taken while it moves (a bump, a gust of wind) are skipped, and stacking goes on once it is still (“Hold the telescope still”). If it then points somewhere else, stacking starts again, or, when most frames are already in, the frames so far are used.
  • Sensitivity: how faint a point of light may be to count as a star. Higher finds more stars but also more noise.
  • If your phone supports it, controls for exposure, ISO, focus and zoom appear here as well.
Diagnostics
A switch under Capture. When it is on, a tap on the status at the bottom left shows the image the last Locate worked with. Tap the image to zoom in.
  • Stack (for a photo: Image): the image Locate used. The local sky background is subtracted before it is stretched, so a brightness gradient from light pollution or the lens no longer shows as a bright disc.
  • Raw: the averaged frames as they came. Sky removed: what is brighter than the sky light, what is darker blue. Detection: the pixels above the threshold.
  • One frame: the first frame alone, to compare with the stack. It exists only for the live camera; a photo is a single image.
  • Marks: the points of light in amber, numbered by brightness, and the matched catalogue stars in green.
  • Below: a histogram with sky level and threshold, and the numbers: frames, camera settings, sky brightness and noise, how much brighter the sky is in the centre than at the edges, threshold, points found, star size for checking the focus, and the result or the reason it failed. Then the last ten attempts.
  • Save image and report saves the layer you are looking at, with the marks if they are on, as PNG, and the numbers as JSON. The Stack without marks can be solved again from the gallery. Copy report puts the numbers on the clipboard, for example to paste into an email.
  • While the live camera runs, a small line shows a quick count of points of light, sky level and star size every 1.5 seconds, handy for focusing.
Compass
Shows the compass correction; Reset compass correction removes it.

The settings you change most often are also in Quick settings (the button at the top of the button column). Red light tints the whole app red to keep your eyes dark-adapted. To go back to the map, tap the camera button while the live camera runs, or ✕ on the photo chip.

Sync Pro

Sync between devices
Keeps the observing list, starred objects, logbook and the telescope settings the same on your phone, tablet and computer. End-to-end encrypted: the data is encrypted on the device with a sync code that never reaches the server, so only your devices can read it.
  • Menu → Sync with other devices opens this tab while sync isn’t set up; once it is, the menu entry syncs at once and shows when it last did.
  • Set up sync on the first device. It shows a QR code.
  • On the next device: Menu → Sync with other devices → Join with a code → Scan code, and hold it up to the QR code. Or send the link (Copy link) to the device and open it there, or paste it into “Or paste the link”. If the telescope settings of the two devices differ, Zenigo asks which ones to use; lists and logbook are always combined.
  • iPhone and iPad: Zenigo from the Home Screen keeps its data apart from Safari. Scan the code inside the app, not with the Camera app (that would open Safari).
  • What syncs: list, starred, logbook (including deletions), location, mount, map filter, focal length, aperture, lowest and highest altitude, eyepieces, Barlow, finder, the rings on the map, eyepiece view orientation and turn. What stays on each device: eyepiece point and pointing (they depend on how that phone sits on the telescope), camera and field of view, compass correction, red light, display and layout.
  • When: at start, when the app comes back to the front, a few seconds after a change, every few minutes while open, and with Sync now. Offline everything works as before; changes are sent once the device is online.
  • Changed on two devices before they synced: different entries and settings are simply combined. A logbook entry edited on both keeps both edits, field by field (note on one, seeing on the other); only a field changed on both takes the later edit. Eyepieces added or removed on each device are combined too. Location (latitude, longitude, where it came from) always comes from one device, the one that changed it last. Deleted on one device and changed on the other: the later of the two counts.
  • Add a device shows the QR code again. Turn off here stops sync on this device and keeps its data. New sync code (after losing a device) moves the group to a new code: add your other devices again. Delete from the server removes the synced copy; your devices keep their data.
  • Keep the code somewhere safe: Add a device → Save as image (the QR code with a short explanation, to print or keep in your photos) or Save as text (link and code, for a password manager). Anyone with it can read and change your synced data, and if all your devices are lost it is the only way back to it.
  • If a device runs an older version of Zenigo than the others, it keeps syncing as long as the data is compatible; otherwise it says so and waits for an update instead of overwriting newer data.
Getting the data back
All you need is the saved code or link.
  • On a new device: open the link, or open Zenigo and go to Menu → Sync with other devices → Join with a code, and scan the saved image or paste the link. The lists, the logbook and the telescope settings come back.
  • Without Zenigo: the small script sync-recover.py (Python 3, needs pip install cryptography) fetches the encrypted copy and decrypts it on your computer: python3 sync-recover.py "<link>" > zenigo-data.json. The result is plain JSON with the observing list, starred objects, logbook entries and settings. The script also describes the encryption, so the data can be read with any other tool too.

Live Pro

Live connection
Connects two devices directly, phone to tablet, phone to phone or tablet to tablet, for example the phone on the telescope and a tablet in your hand. There is no server and no internet involved: the devices exchange their connection details as two QR codes and then talk to each other over the local network.
  • What it needs: both devices in the same Wi-Fi, or one in the other’s hotspot (it also works without internet, as long as the hotspot can be switched on). On iPhone and iPad the Personal Hotspot needs mobile data switched on. Two devices that are each online with their own SIM card can’t reach each other this way: put one into the other’s hotspot.
  • Step 1, the role: Menu → Live connection (or Settings → Live) asks what this device does. At the telescope: it sits on the telescope and shares where it points. Follow: it is in your hand and shows where the other device’s telescope points. Each device keeps its choice (at first, phones are at the telescope and tablets and computers follow). Then Next.
  • Step 2, options and Connect: only the options for that role: at the telescope Send the camera image, when following Show the telescope’s camera image (see below). Then Connect. The device at the telescope shows a code; the following device scans it and then shows its own code, which you hold in front of the first device’s camera (“1 of 2”, “2 of 2”). A second or two later both say “Connected”. Do this before the phone goes into its holder.
  • Same role on both: if the device at the telescope sees the first code of another device at the telescope, it offers Follow it instead and connects right away. If both are set to Follow, neither shows a code: change one to At the telescope. Once connected, Change role in Settings → Live switches the role; if both have the same role, the settings say so.
  • What is shared: where the telescope points, from the device that knows it (camera, Align or compass), about ten times a second at most and only when it moves; and the target: choosing or clearing it on one device does the same on the other. Nothing else, unless you turn on the camera image (next point).
  • Camera image (optional, off by default): the following device can show the telescope’s camera image instead of the map, with the same stars, crosshair, rings and arrow on it as on the telescope. On the device on the telescope turn on Send the camera image (Settings → Live) and start its camera; on the following device turn on Show the telescope’s camera image, or tap the camera button in its pill (it appears while the other device can send). The image goes directly to the other device, like everything else, scaled down to about 540 lines at 15 frames a second; while the following device shows the map, no frames are sent. It uses more battery on the telescope side and more Wi-Fi; following works the same without it. The stars on the image come from the telescope’s last solution, so they appear once it has located (or aligned). Dragging, Show on map and the like switch to the map as before; Follow the telescope again brings the image back. Both devices need this version of Zenigo.
  • While connected: on the following device a pill says “Following the telescope” (on phones on the right above the buttons, on larger screens at the bottom right next to Locate); on the device on the telescope the pill “Live · another device follows” sits with the pills on the left, which can be pushed aside. Tap a pill for the settings; its ✕ ends the connection on both devices. The menu entry shows it too.
  • Pausing: as soon as you turn the following device’s map yourself (drag it, Show on map, …), it stops following. The telescope’s crosshair stays where the telescope points, with the directions and the arrow to the target from there. Follow the telescope again (with the pills on the left) turns the map back and follows again.
  • Off while following: camera, Locate, Continuous, Align, the eyepiece point, the setup assistant, night photo and photos from the gallery belong to the device on the telescope; on the following device they are greyed out (a tap says why).
  • Following: the following device, as long as it doesn’t point by itself, turns its map to the other device’s telescope, shows the crosshair there and gives the directions to the target from it, also with sound. The label at the bottom left says “Live · following the telescope”, or “Live · the telescope’s camera” while it shows the camera image. Once the device points by itself (camera, Align), its own pointing counts.
  • Lost connection: when a device is locked, goes out of Wi-Fi range or the app is closed, the connection ends and both say so; connect again with the codes. Disconnect ends it on both devices.
  • The codes are valid for this one connection. The connection is encrypted, and only the device that has seen the code can connect.

Offline and updates

Once added to the home screen and opened online, Zenigo works completely offline: star catalogue (including the deeper one with stars to magnitude 9 and the NGC/IC objects, and the extended catalogue once downloaded), calculations, solving, observing list and logbook all run on the phone. Only the satellites need a connection. When a new version is available, a message “A new version of Zenigo is available” appears with an Update button. You can ignore it; the current version keeps working until you update. If no message appears, close the app completely and open it again.

Camera and sensor access

Zenigo asks for the camera (for Locate) and, on iPhone, for motion and orientation access (for Align). If you declined once, the browser remembers it and won’t ask again; a web page can’t open the settings for you either. When this happens, the app shows the steps for your device and a button to reload. Here they are in full:

iPhone and iPad

  • Camera, in Safari: tap aA (or the page icon) on the left of the address bar → Website Settings → set Camera to Ask or Allow. Then reload the page.
  • Camera, app on the home screen: open the Settings app → Apps → Safari → Camera → Ask or Allow. If that doesn’t help, remove Zenigo from the home screen and add it again from Safari.
  • Motion and compass: iPhone remembers a refusal only until the tab or Safari is closed. Close the Zenigo tab (or quit Safari, or close the app completely) and open it again, then tap Align and choose Allow. If your iOS version shows it, also check Settings → Apps → Safari → Motion & Orientation Access.

Android

  • In Chrome: tap the icon on the left of the address bar → Permissions → allow Camera and Motion sensors, or tap Reset permissions. Then reload.
  • App on the home screen: long-press the Zenigo icon → App info → Permissions, or in Chrome → ⋮ → Settings → Site settings → Camera / Motion sensors.
  • Blocked motion sensors don’t produce an error, they just send nothing. If Align shows nothing after a moment, the app assumes they are blocked and shows these steps.

Computer

  • Click the icon on the left of the address bar (Chrome, Edge) or open the site settings (Safari → Settings → Websites → Camera) and allow the camera for Zenigo. Then reload.

Troubleshooting

The camera doesn’t start (“AbortError”, “NotReadableError”)
The browser couldn’t open the camera, usually because something else has it. Zenigo already tries again with simpler settings. If it still fails: close other apps and browser tabs that use the camera; on an iPad leave Split View and Slide Over (iPadOS doesn’t give the camera to a browser there); make sure Zenigo isn’t open twice, in Safari and from the Home Screen; then close Zenigo completely and open it again. If you chose a camera in Settings → Camera & sensors, try “Back camera (automatic)”. Different from a blocked camera, which is described under Camera and sensor access.
“Only a few stars detected”
It isn’t dark enough, the area is too empty or the camera exposes too briefly. Aim at a part of the sky with brighter stars, raise Frames to stack and Sensitivity, and if your phone offers it, a longer exposure or higher ISO in Settings → Camera & sensors → Capture. Still too dark? Take a night photo instead: the camera app exposes for seconds. To see why, switch on Diagnostics (Settings → Camera & sensors → Capture) and tap the status after a Locate. Make sure the camera is focused and the lens is clean and not fogged up.
“Field of view not found” or “No match”
If the camera looks through a mirror or prism, switch on Mirrored image. Otherwise get more open sky into the image. If you changed camera or zoom, tap Measure again in Settings → Camera & sensors → Camera field of view. A wrong search range there can also prevent the first measurement.
The directions are off although the map looks right
Check the location in Settings → General → Location, and set the eyepiece point again. It is wrong as soon as the holder has moved.
Compass mode is far off
Normal near a metal tube. Accept the compass correction after a locate, or move the phone away from metal. Locating makes it exact anyway.
The view flips or rotates when pointing high
Turn on the phone’s rotation lock (iPhone: Control Centre; Android: quick settings). Lying flat, the phone can’t tell portrait from landscape.
The map jumps or shakes
Strong magnetic disturbances or fast jerks. After a locate the gyroscope takes over and the view becomes calm.
Camera not available
The camera access was declined. See Camera and sensor access for the steps on your device. If the camera is used by another app, close that app first.
“This device provides no orientation data”
The motion sensors are blocked or missing. See Camera and sensor access.
Locating takes very long
The first locate with a new camera or a gallery photo searches the field of view, which can take a while. The Locate button turns into Stop: tap it to cancel, then try with more sky in the image or a narrower search range in Settings → Camera & sensors → Camera field of view.
No satellites on the map
They need an internet connection and are hidden offline. Check that “ISS and bright satellites” is on in the Filter; the Solar system and Deep sky presets switch them off.
The eyepiece view shows hardly any stars
It shows stars to magnitude 9, with the extended catalogue to 11 (as far as your aperture reaches). In a narrow field away from the Milky Way that can be only a few; through the telescope you see many more. The pattern of the brighter ones is what helps to find the field.
The app shows an old version
Tap Update when the message appears, or close the app completely and open it again.

Licence key
After buying, the thank-you page shows your key (it starts with ZP1.) and gives it to Zenigo in the same browser. On other devices, and in Zenigo from the Home Screen on an iPhone or iPad (it keeps its data apart from Safari), open Menu › Zenigo Pro › Enter licence key and paste it, or open the link Open Zenigo with Pro there. The key works on all devices you use yourself and is checked on the device, without a connection. For sync, each device needs the key.
Lost licence key
Zenigo keeps the key on the device, so deleting Zenigo, or clearing the browser’s website data, removes it. To get it back:
  • From a backup: every backup made while you had Pro holds the key. Restore it in Settings › General › Backup and restore, and Pro is back.
  • From your email: if you sent the key to yourself (Email it to me on the thank-you page, or Menu › Zenigo Pro › Email my key to me), open the link in that email or paste the key.
  • With your order reference: the thank-you page shows it (it starts with txn_). zenigo.app/pro/welcome/?txn= followed by it shows your key again.
  • Otherwise: write to zenigo@christianranz.com from the email address you bought with. We find the order at Paddle and send you the link to your key.
A refunded licence can’t be shown again.
After 12 months
The licence is paid once and does not renew by itself. 30 days before the end Zenigo reminds you. When it ends, the Pro features switch off; your lists, logbook and settings stay, and sync stops. Buy Pro again (Menu › Zenigo Pro › Renew) to continue: bought before the end, the new 12 months start when the current ones end.
